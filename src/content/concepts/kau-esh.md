the kau-esh are the little kahu. the term covers two overlapping groups: those who are in <a class="wiki-link" href="/organizations/zou-esh">zou-esh</a> training but have not yet completed it, and those who began the training and did not finish, the washouts. <span class="kt">kau</span> reads as a diminutive, a smaller or incomplete form, set against the full <span class="kt"><a class="wiki-link" href="/concepts/kahu">kahu</a></span>. a kau-esh is one who is on the path of the order, or was, without having arrived.

the term is not an insult. the zou-esh order is the hardest path in <a class="wiki-link" href="/concepts/kahu">kahu</a> life and the narrowest. only around one in ten students is even asked to try for it, only one or two in a hundred of those make it through screening, and of those who enter actual training, a large proportion do not complete the minimum fifteen years. washing out is the expected outcome, not the shameful one. the order is built knowing that most who begin will not finish.

what a kau-esh becomes is part of why the term carries no shame. being accepted into the path at all sets a person up for other roles across kahu society. the kau-esh carry partial training, internal knowledge of the order, and skills the rest of the kahu do not have, and they are scattered through the working life of the caldera and the plateau in vocations that benefit from what they learned before they left the path. a kau-esh is not a failed zou-esh. a kau-esh is a kahu who walked far enough up the order's road to be changed by it, and then took what they had gained into the wider life of the people.

the kau-esh should not be confused with the zou-esh, the active operators of the order who have completed training. nor with the retired zou-esh of the <a class="wiki-link" href="/organizations/the-council-of-the-tapestry">council of watchers</a>, who completed the full path and finished their active service. the kau-esh are the ones still climbing, and the ones who climbed partway and stepped off. the precise line between the two, and whether the order uses the term formally or chiefly in everyday speech, are open canon.
