importance-first syntax is the foundational syntactic principle of kahu-tok. the most important element of a sentence leads. emphasis, focus, and the speaker's actual point are visible from the first word.

the principle is structural rather than stylistic. it is built into the language's grammar. when a kahu speaker constructs a sentence, the leading position is reserved for whatever element of the utterance carries the most weight in the current context. that element may be the subject, the object, the verb, a temporal marker, a modifier, depending on what the sentence is actually about. the speaker's choice of leading element communicates as much as the content of the sentence itself.

this is the opposite of the english pattern, in which fixed word order (subject-verb-object) means that the most important element of an english sentence is usually buried in the middle and must be inferred from emphasis, intonation, or surrounding context. in kahu-tok, no inference is needed. the structure tells the listener what matters.

the principle has direct consequences for kahu social life. emphasis is harder to hide. a speaker who is trying to obscure what they mean by burying it in a sentence has fewer grammatical tools available. a kahu listener does not have to wait for the speaker to finish to know what the speaker thinks is at stake. arguments, agreements, and decisions move faster because the topology of the discussion is visible from the opening words of each contribution.

the name <span class="proper">tora vyl drak</span> is an example of importance-first construction at the level of a phrase. the words read hope, call, last in their kahu-tok order. the most important element, hope, leads. the qualification, that it is the call of the last attempt, follows. an english speaker would naturally render the phrase as "the hope of the last call" or "the last call's hope," moving the important element to a syntactic position that the english listener would parse as the head. the kahu construction is the more direct one: it places the important element first.

bixel's design of kahu-tok was guided by the philosophical commitment that the language should not allow its speakers to confuse themselves or each other about what they actually meant. importance-first syntax is one of the central tools bixel used to make that commitment operational. the other principal tool is the refusal to encode scale distinctions in the word <span class="kt">kahu</span>. between them, these two design decisions shape almost every kahu utterance.
