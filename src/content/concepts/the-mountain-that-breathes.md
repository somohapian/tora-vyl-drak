the mountain that breathes is the kahu's geothermal plume as it appeared to distant survivor pockets across the dark century. the kahu themselves call the source of the plume hart-ston, the sacred volcanic cone within the caldera. but to survivors elsewhere on the post-fracture earth, who could see the plume rising from the cascade arc on freezing winter mornings but could not see the people below it, the plume was a singular feature of the landscape: a mountain that was warm enough to breathe steam into the cold sky.

the legend had practical effects. survivors who had been scattered across continents by the fracture and isolated by the silence had little reason to undertake long journeys, and the mountain that breathes provided one. across the centuries after the great eruption formed the caldera, word of the plume traveled along the slow trade networks that had survived the collapse. it reached the russian far east, the canadian arctic, central asia, and eventually anywhere that pre-collapse populations had survived in concentrations large enough to maintain even rudimentary information exchange.

the legend was always partly accurate and partly inaccurate. the plume was real. the warmth was real. what the legend made of the people who lived beneath the plume varied. in some traditions they became long-lived god-like inhabitants, drawn from elements of pre-collapse mythology that had survived in the survivor populations. in others they became more practical figures, refugees who had found a way to survive the conditions that had killed everyone else and might share what they knew. in the russian far east, the place itself was renamed across the legend's transmission into <span class="proper">tura vyl drak</span>, "the last dragon's hope" or "the valley of the second sun," a folk etymology that read the kahu-tok roots through a different linguistic lens and produced a name the actual kahu did not recognize.

the kahu's messenger missions during the age of tapestry both produced the legend and depended on it. the zou-esh ancestors who went out along the cascade arc and beyond were spreading word, but the word also traveled without them through the relay of survivor-to-survivor trade. by the time a long-distance migration arrived at the gate, like the yamantau descendants who crossed the bering ice bridge, the migrants knew both the legend they had set out to find and, by then, more practical information about what they were actually approaching.

the plume is still visible. hart-ston still produces steam on cold mornings. the legend has faded among the few outside populations that still exist, partly because those populations have largely been brought in by zou-esh missions and have integrated into the kahu, and partly because the world below the rim holds far fewer people than it did during the age of tapestry. the mountain that breathes is now primarily a piece of kahu self-knowledge, a record of how the kahu appeared to other populations when those populations existed.
