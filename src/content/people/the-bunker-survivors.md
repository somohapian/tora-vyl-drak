the bunker survivors are the surviving populations of the deep mountain bunkers in the cascade arc who rode out the dark century underground and became the founding generation of the kahu. they are the ancestors. everything the kahu now are descends, in part, from what these people survived and how they survived it.

the principal bunker locations were in the idaho rockies and the blue mountains of eastern oregon. these were not the longs' arcologies. these were the bunkers of preppers, of rural communities that had built community-scale shelters, and of continuity-of-government personnel who had been assigned to deep-mountain installations as the pre-collapse state's contingency planning. when the fracture came, these populations were inside, sealed, and at sufficient elevation that the immediate impact effects did not destroy them.

bunker life across the dark century was, in the kahu's own account, structured by the failure curve of pre-collapse technology and the parallel rise of biological adaptation. the engineering inheritance of the bunker generation gave way over the first hundred years; the social inheritance gave way across the second century. children of bunker-life were not the same people their bunker-builder ancestors had been. by 2283 ad, the populations that descended were demographically and culturally distinct from the populations that had entered.

what they had developed underground that they brought with them was substantial. <span class="kt">ston-shen</span>, the chemosynthetic lichen-bacteria paste cultivated on geothermal vents, was the food technology that made survival possible past the failure of the rations. the bastard tongue, tok-peles, was the speech they shared. early fungiculture and underground livestock practices that would mature into kahu agriculture were established. small-community living patterns under conditions of resource scarcity were practiced for so long that they became second nature; the kahu's communal living patterns are direct inheritance from bunker life.

the different bunker populations were not identical. the prepper bunkers, the rural community shelters, and the continuity-of-government installations carried different cultural baselines from their pre-collapse origins. across the dark century these distinctions softened under the shared conditions of survival, but they did not entirely disappear. the founding generation of the kahu was a composite community that took two more centuries, the age of tapestry, to fully integrate into a single culture.

the bunker survivors do not appear in the kahu record as named individuals except in fragments. the documentation tradition that the kahu would later develop did not exist underground. what is known of the bunker survivors comes from oral traditions preserved into the tapestry, from later archeological recovery of bunker sites by zou-esh expeditions, and from inferences drawn by kahu historians working with the materials available. the kahu's pride in their ancestors is real and is qualified by the recognition that most of what these people lived through has been lost.
