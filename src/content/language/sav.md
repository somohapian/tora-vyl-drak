<span class="kt">sav</span> is the kahu-tok verb for to know. it comes from the spanish <span class="kt">saber</span>, carried in by the hearth-tongue speakers of the founding generations and regularized by <a class="wiki-link" href="/people/bixel">bixel</a> into the engineered core.

the word exists because of a collision. in early <span class="kt"><a class="wiki-link" href="/language/kahu-tok">kahu-tok</a></span>, the single word <span class="kt"><a class="wiki-link" href="/language/no">no</a></span> carried both the negation, "no," and the verb "to know." this made the most ordinary admission in any language, "i do not know," very hard to say cleanly: a speaker reaching for "i don't know" was reaching for the same syllable twice, in opposite senses. the language repaired itself the way living languages do, by reaching for a word already in the room. the hearth-tongue had <span class="kt">saber</span>. <span class="kt">sav</span> took over the verb, and <span class="kt">no</span> was left to do the one job a negation should do.

so the modern form is clean. <span class="kt">no</span> negates. <span class="kt">sav</span> knows. "mi no sav" is "i do not know," and it says itself without tangling. the fix touched nothing else in the language; it only gave knowing its own root.

the one place the older sense survives is a proper name. the scientist track of the <a class="wiki-link" href="/organizations/zou-esh">zou-esh</a>, the knowers, is still called the No, the track of those who go out to learn what the world has become. there the homophone with the negation causes no trouble, because the track-name is always clear from context. in ordinary speech, to know is <span class="kt">sav</span>.

whether <span class="kt">sav</span> distinguishes knowing a fact from knowing a person or knowing how to do a thing, and how it sits beside <span class="kt">think</span> in the kahu's understanding of mind, are open canon.
