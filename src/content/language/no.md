<span class="kt">no</span> is the kahu-tok word for no. it is the answer to a yes-or-no question and the grammatical negation that turns a statement around. it is one of the foundational words of the <a class="wiki-link" href="/language/kahu-tok">kahu-tok</a> core.

in the earliest form of the language, <span class="kt">no</span> did double duty: it was both the negation and the verb "to know." this produced a famous tangle, since "i do not know" came out as the same small word said twice in opposite senses. the language settled the matter by moving the verb onto its own root, <span class="kt"><a class="wiki-link" href="/language/sav">sav</a></span>, from the hearth-tongue. <span class="kt">no</span> kept the negation. the repair touched nothing else.

so <span class="kt">no</span> today does one thing, and does it plainly. it answers, and it negates. "mi no sav," i do not know. "no, mi no zou," no, i am not going. the cleanness is the point: a word that means too many things at once is a word that lets a speaker fool themselves, and <a class="wiki-link" href="/people/bixel">bixel</a> built the language to make that harder, not easier.

the one survival of the old breadth is a proper name. the scientist track of the <a class="wiki-link" href="/organizations/zou-esh">zou-esh</a> is called the No, the knowers who go out to study the world. the name is a fossil of the time <span class="kt">no</span> also meant to know, and it causes no confusion, because a track-name is always clear from where it stands.

whether kahu-tok marks softer and harder degrees of negation is open canon.
