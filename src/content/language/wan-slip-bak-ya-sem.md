<span class="kt">wan-slip-bak, ya-sem</span> is a kahu-tok idiom for the kind of memory that has not yet become the past. literally it is "one sleep back, now-same": a thing that happened a sleep ago and still feels like now. a kahu reaches for it when a loss is too recent to have settled, or a joy too fresh to have faded, or simply when yesterday refuses to move off into memory and stays pressed up against the present.

the phrase is a fossil of how the ancestors counted time. in the long dark of the bunkers, before <a class="wiki-link" href="/events/the-first-descent">the first descent</a>, the survivors lived underground with no sun to mark the days, and so they counted in sleeps. a span of time was so many <span class="kt">slip</span>, so many sleeps. the descent gave the <a class="wiki-link" href="/concepts/kahu">kahu</a> the sun back, and the day became <span class="kt">sun</span> again, but the old counting never fully left the language. it survives in idiom, and <span class="kt">wan-slip-bak, ya-sem</span> is the warmest example: a way of measuring nearness in sleeps rather than days, kept because some things are better measured that way.

the idiom carries the language's refusal to treat time as only a line. <span class="kt"><a class="wiki-link" href="/language/kahu-tok">kahu-tok</a></span> marks past and future by tone on the verb, cleanly, when a speaker wants to be exact. but exactness is not always what a speaker wants. <span class="kt">wan-slip-bak, ya-sem</span> is for the moment when the grammar's clean past is wrong, when yesterday is, in the way that matters, still now.

whether the idiom has a clipped everyday form, and whether it leans toward warmth or toward grief, are open canon. likely it does both, and the difference is in the voice.
